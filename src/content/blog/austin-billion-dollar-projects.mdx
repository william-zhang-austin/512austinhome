More than $50 billion is being spent or planned on seven projects around Austin, and the biggest one isn't downtown at all. It's a Samsung chip plant about 30 miles northeast of the city, in Taylor. I'm an Austin real estate agent with eXp Realty, and I list homes across the metro, so these Austin billion-dollar projects come up in almost every seller conversation I have.

Here's the quick version, as of November 2026. TxDOT is sinking I-35 below downtown for $4.5 billion. Light rail is planned at $7.1 billion or more. The convention center is being rebuilt for $1.6 billion, the airport is expanding, and Tesla's Giga Texas plant still anchors the southeast. Waterline is now the tallest building in Texas, and Samsung started at $17 billion.

Here's the catch. Money headed into a region doesn't mean every neighborhood gets more expensive. In recent closings, homes with an Austin address were roughly flat from a year earlier, while most of the surrounding suburbs sold for less. Both things are true at once. Below, I'll go through each project: what it costs, where it sits and when it's supposed to finish. This post covers the seven biggest by dollars. For the longer countdown of everything changing the city, see [20 major projects changing Austin](/blog/austin-major-development-projects/).

## 1. The I-35 rebuild and the cap and stitch plan

TxDOT is spending $4.5 billion to rebuild I-35 from Airport Boulevard down to Lady Bird Lake. Ground broke in October 2024. Through downtown, the main lanes drop about 25 feet below ground, the upper decks you've driven under for years come out, and two free HOV lanes are added over about eight miles. The rebuild is expected to wrap up in the early 2030s.

The part people get excited about is what could go on top. It's called cap and stitch. A cap is a park built on a lid over the highway. A stitch is a wide bridge that feels like a normal street, so you can walk across I-35 without feeling like you're crossing a freeway.

In May 2025, city council voted 8 to 2 (with one abstention) to commit up to $104 million for the base of three downtown caps and two stitches to the north. That's a $41 million state loan plus $63 million in city debt.

The full price is where it gets shaky. Depending on who's counting, the caps and stitches run somewhere between $600 million and $900 million, and the estimates keep moving. A $105 million federal grant for the caps was canceled. As of this spring, no private money had come in, and the city's finance director said she couldn't recommend going ahead. Mayor Kirk Watson later tried to cut the plan to a single cap and didn't have the votes, so three caps and two stitches are still on the table.

TxDOT set November 2026 as the deadline for the caps to make its 2028 bid package. If they miss that package, the next clean chance could be as far off as 2043, so check the latest council and TxDOT news before you count on any of the parks.

If you own near the highway, count on the rebuild, because that's happening. Treat the parks as a bonus. The homes that feel it most sit on the seam: the east side of downtown, Rainey Street and the neighborhoods just east of I-35. And the city's share of the cap money is debt, which gets repaid, so it's worth knowing [how Austin property taxes work](/blog/austin-property-tax-guide-2026/).

## 2. Project Connect light rail

Project Connect's first phase is light rail: 9.8 miles and 15 stations, running from UT through downtown with a brand new bridge over Lady Bird Lake. The Austin Transit Partnership puts the cost at $7.1 billion. One report citing federal numbers, from Planetizen in May, puts it closer to $8.2 billion.

This year it finally started moving. The environmental study was finished in January 2026. Austin Rail Constructors was picked as the main builder in February, Kiewit was picked for the maintenance yard in April, and Stadler was picked to build the train cars in July. Construction is planned to start in 2027, with the first riders in 2033.

I'll be straight about the federal piece. The Federal Transit Administration gave the project a Medium-High rating, which is a good sign. But as of now there's no signed funding agreement, and until there is, I'd treat those dates as a plan.

For a homeowner near a planned station, this is a ten-year story, not a next-year bump. If you're out in Pflugerville or Round Rock, it isn't a train you'll be walking to anytime soon. Most of those commutes still run on roads, and my [Austin commute and traffic guide](/blog/austin-commute-traffic-guide-2026/) covers that side.

## 3. The Austin Convention Center rebuild

The Austin Convention Center closed after South by Southwest in 2025. The city is rebuilding it for $1.6 billion across six downtown blocks, and the goal is to reopen in time for South by in 2029.

The new building is a lot bigger. Rentable space goes from 365,000 square feet to 620,000, about 70 percent more. The city projects about $750 million a year in economic impact from the new center, compared with about $470 million from the old one. That means more people downtown on weekdays, filling hotels and restaurants.

The tradeoff is the wait. If you own a condo looking down on the site, you're looking at a construction zone until 2029.

## 4. The AUS airport expansion

Austin-Bergstrom had 21.7 million passengers in 2025, its third busiest year ever. October 2025 was the busiest month in its history, a little over 2 million people. News reports put the expansion program at about $5 billion, with systems built for the demand of 30 million passengers a year. The airport says the total is still in development.

The first piece you'll see is already under way. At the end of September 2026, the airport broke ground on Concourse M, a temporary concourse with six gates, set to open in 2028. The bigger piece comes after that: Concourse B, with 26 gates.

Here's the part most people don't know. None of this is paid for with local Austin tax dollars. It's airport revenue, revenue bonds and FAA grants. Unlike the highway caps, this one isn't coming back through your taxes.

An airport this busy pulls jobs, hotels and warehouses toward it, and a lot of that land sits east and southeast of the runway, out toward Del Valle and SH 130.

## 5. Tesla Giga Texas

Tesla's Giga Texas sits in southeast Travis County along SH 130, near Del Valle. Tesla has put well over $5 billion into it.

But 2025 was a rough year. Based on Tesla's report to the county, the workforce went from about 21,000 people to about 16,500, a 22 percent drop in one year. In April 2026, Travis County held back 9 percent of a Tesla tax rebate because the paperwork wasn't complete.

Even after losing almost 5,000 jobs, Giga Texas is still the largest employer in Travis County. If you own in Del Valle or along the SH 130 corridor, you lean on this plant. When it's hiring, a lot of people want a home close to work. Leaning that hard on one employer is also a risk, and you just saw what a single year can do.

## 6. Waterline and the south shore

Near Rainey Street, Waterline is now the tallest building in Texas at more than 1,000 feet. The 1 Hotel inside it opened on September 3, 2026.

What surprised me is what comes next. In January, the Downtown Austin Alliance said no new downtown construction was set to break ground in 2026. To me that says the market is still catching up with what's already built, not that it's failing.

Look across the lake and One Lady Bird Lake is going up on the south shore, about 300 homes, set to finish in early 2028. Right next to it is the old Statesman site on South Congress: about 19 acres, with nearly 1,400 homes, 1.5 million square feet of offices, a 275-room hotel and parks along the shoreline.

The city approved that plan in December 2022. It got sued, and in June 2025 a judge upheld the approval. As far as I can find, there's still no start date. It's approved and cleared in court, and it's still waiting.

Bouldin, Travis Heights and South River City sit closest. When the south shore fills in, those neighborhoods get the parks and restaurants. They also get more people and more traffic.

## 7. Samsung Taylor

Drive about 30 miles northeast of downtown and you reach Samsung's chip plant in Taylor. It started as a $17 billion investment, and Samsung's full Texas plan is more than double that. The federal government agreed to put in about $4.7 billion through the CHIPS Act.

The tools moved in during April 2026. These are the machines that make 2-nanometer chips. Samsung hasn't announced its first chips yet, and reports put full production in 2027. Samsung also has a deal worth at least $16.5 billion to make AI chips for Tesla.

It isn't stopping at one plant. Samsung says it plans to start building a second one by the end of 2026, with production targeted for 2030. The plan projects about 1,800 direct jobs in the first decade.

Two cautions. This plant was supposed to be running by the end of 2024, and the date slipped several times. And Taylor is a small market, so just a few sales can move the numbers in either direction. Still, in recent closings Taylor sat at the bottom of the metro on price, which puts the biggest check on this list right next to some of the cheapest homes in the region. I'm not telling you Taylor is a buy. It's a wait-and-see. If you own in Taylor, Hutto or Manor, this plant is the thing to watch.

## What this means for your home value in Austin

Here's how I read it. Projects inside the city may help hold up prices in the middle. Some of the cheapest homes, in Taylor, Manor and Hutto, sit closest to the biggest money going in outside the core. In recent closings, most suburbs sold for less than a year earlier even with all of this on the way, and buyers had room to negotiate. That gap is the story. For the longer view on why suburb prices have been sliding, I wrote about it in [why Austin suburb home prices are dropping](/blog/why-austin-suburb-home-prices-are-dropping/).

Timing matters too. The stretch between approval and opening is often when a project starts showing up in home values, and once it's built, a lot of that is already in the number. Concourse M opens in 2028, the convention center in 2029, Samsung's second plant is targeted for 2030 and the trains for 2033. Most of those windows are still open. I'm not predicting what that does to prices, and it cuts both ways: a park or a station nearby can help, while years of construction and repayment of borrowed money can weigh.
