In the spring of 1963, a state highway crew was drilling test holes in Georgetown for a new I-35 overpass. The drill bit went through solid limestone, then dropped more than 20 feet into nothing. They'd hit a cave nobody knew was there. It became Inner Space Cavern, and it's one reason Austin caves and tunnels are worth knowing about if you own a home here.

I'm William Zhang, an Austin real estate agent with eXp Realty, and I list homes across the metro. Underground rules rarely come up at a listing appointment, but they shape what can be built next door, which flood map your street is on and what a buyer asks about. ## 1. Inner Space Cavern: an Ice Age cave beside I-35

TxDOT says its crews hit the cave several times during that 1963 drilling. That November, a group of cave explorers went down and, in a few days, mapped more than a mile of passages. It opened to the public in the summer of 1966. Today it sits on the I-35 frontage road in Georgetown.

The bones are the real story. Scientists have found more than 40 kinds of animals in the cave, and 11 of them are extinct. They include a mammoth, a saber-toothed cat and a glyptodont, which is basically an armadillo the size of a small car. One bone has been dated at about 23,000 years old, according to the Texas Speleological Survey. That's the oldest radiocarbon-dated bone from a Texas cave.

In 2025, the same agency that drilled into it sent a robot back in. TxDOT spent 18 days over four months scanning the cave with a laser-mapping robot named Dot, so future I-35 plans account for it.

This isn't a Georgetown oddity. The rock under much of Williamson County is limestone full of holes, and the Williamson County Conservation Foundation said in 2018 that the county has well over 700 known caves. ## 2. The bugs that can stop a bulldozer

The Bone Cave harvestman is tiny, pale and has no eyes. It lives only in caves across roughly 150 square miles of Travis and Williamson counties, and it's been federally endangered for more than 30 years. The conservation foundation said in 2018 that this one species had turned up in at least 70 caves in Round Rock alone.

Caves aren't only out in the country. In February 2018, a hole opened up under Cambria Drive in the Brushy Creek area, and there was a cave with several rooms beneath it. That summer, crews found a fifth room nobody knew about, and work stopped until the state signed off on a plan. In 2019, crews building a MoPac bridge at La Crosse Avenue in South Austin ran into dozens of caves.

For builders in Williamson County, there's a voluntary program for this. Under the county's regional habitat plan, which started in 2008, a cave with endangered species gets a protective circle of about 345 feet. A builder can build outside it or pay a fee to build inside, and the program gets a permit in about 60 days instead of about 2 years. As of 2024, 139 properties had signed up, per Community Impact. It's one reason some land around the north suburbs stays green.

It isn't settled, either. Landowners have been pushing to take the harvestman off the endangered list since 2014, and as of the last federal decision in 2022, it stays listed. None of this means your lot has a cave. If you're curious, ask a qualified inspector or geotechnical engineer.

## 3. The river under South Austin

Barton Springs Pool in Zilker Park is 3 acres of water that stays around 68 to 70 degrees all year, and it comes up from underground. It's fed by the Edwards Aquifer, a limestone system under parts of southern Travis and Hays counties. Two endangered salamanders live in the springs: the Barton Springs salamander, listed in 1997, and the Austin blind salamander, listed in 2013.

That's why the Save Our Springs ordinance exists. Austin voters passed it in August 1992 with about 64% of the vote. It limits how much of a lot can be covered by roofs, driveways and parking. Published sources describe a 15% cap over the recharge zone, where water soaks in, and up to 25% farther out.

The fight didn't end in 1992. In 1993, the legislature passed a bill to void the ordinance, and Governor Ann Richards vetoed it. The Texas Supreme Court upheld it in 1998. More recently, a 2023 state law lets landowners outside the city limits petition to be released from the city's extraterritorial rules. By fall 2024, Austin staff told the Austin Monitor that about 16% of owners in the Barton Springs zone had gotten out, and that number was rising. Those owners follow the county's smaller rulebook instead.

Here's the odd part. In early November 2026, Lake Travis was nearly full, with TWDB listing it at 94.8% and LCRA at 98%. Yet the Barton Springs/Edwards Aquifer Conservation District has had the aquifer in drought since mid-2022. Full lakes on top, a thirsty aquifer underneath.

Circle C in southwest Austin sits in the Barton Springs Zone, and its developer sued the city over these rules in the 1990s. Today there's no big new-home pipeline in that area, while southeast Austin has plenty of new homes. I can't prove the aquifer rules cause that, because those southwest neighborhoods also have older homes and bigger lots. But if you're selling there, you're mostly competing against your neighbors, not a builder down the road offering a new house.

If you own in or near the zone and want to add on, pave a bigger driveway or build a pool, check with the City of Austin's Watershed Protection Department before you plan anything. I can't tell you how the ordinance applies to your lot, and neither can a blog post.

## 4. The tunnel under downtown

Under Waterloo Park, just north of 12th Street, a flood-control tunnel runs about 5,600 feet to Lady Bird Lake. It's 22 to 26 feet wide and sits 70 feet down. When Waller Creek floods, water drops into it and comes out at the lake. In dry weather, it pumps lake water back up to keep the creek flowing.

It cost more than $160 million, and the growth around it helped pay for it through new property taxes from the district. According to KUT's reporting and the city, it pulled about 28 acres of downtown out of the 100-year floodplain, and FEMA's maps have reflected that since 2022. If you own near a creek, which side of the floodplain line your street is on is part of what a buyer will weigh.

Waterloo Park reopened in 2021. The Confluence, 13 acres of new park by the lake, opened in June 2026 and cost $91.5 million. Waterline, the tower beside the creek, opened in September 2026. It's more than 1,000 feet tall, the tallest building in Texas.

The tunnel had problems, too. In 2018, the city called its own tunnel "defective" and demanded about $22 million back from the contractor, who sued. The city says it still handles a 100-year storm. It also ran about two years late, partly because of a building a few blocks away: the Capitol.

## 5. The Capitol you can't build in front of

The Texas Capitol Extension opened in January 1993. It's four stories underground, more than 600,000 square feet, with big skylights and tunnels to other state office buildings. If you've walked the north lawn, you were standing on top of it.

Above ground, state law has protected views of the Capitol since 1983, and the city added its own rules soon after. Think of invisible lines running from spots around town to the dome. With a few exceptions, nothing can be built into them, which is why some downtown towers narrow or step back partway up.

The Waller Creek tunnel's inlet was built too tall for one of those lines. Work stopped in 2014, and part of it had to be rebuilt. The lines can move, too: in 2025, the state removed some of them over the old Erwin Center site so UT can build its medical campus there.

## 6. The subway that never got dug

In November 2020, Austin voters approved Project Connect, including a downtown subway with underground stations. Then the price arrived. The tunnel grew from about 1.5 miles to more than 4 miles, and its cost went from about $2 billion to more than $4 billion. KUT reported that flood risk and the Capitol view rules were part of the reason. The whole light rail plan went from $5.8 billion to $10.3 billion.

The subway was cut in the summer of 2023. What's left is 9.8 miles of light rail with 15 stations, no subway, and a price tag above $8.2 billion. Construction is supposed to start in 2027, with first trains targeted for 2033.

About half the money is supposed to come from Washington, and as of fall 2026, none of it has been awarded. A lawsuit over how the project is being paid for is also still going. If you own near the route, it's worth watching. I wouldn't price a house off a 2033 date. ## 7. The tunnel being dug right now

I-35 through central Austin is being rebuilt as a $4.5 billion project, and between Holly Street and Airport Boulevard the main lanes are being lowered as much as 60 feet. A sunken highway is basically a ditch, and water runs downhill. So TxDOT is boring 6.5 miles of tunnels, 22 feet wide (big enough for a bus), for $748 million.

The tunnels run under I-35 from 45th Street down to Cesar Chavez, then east under Cesar Chavez toward US 183. There are nine shafts, some up to 200 feet deep. Two tunnel boring machines were built in Germany, and the plan is to start the first one near Airport Boulevard. A new pump station will move up to 130,000 gallons of water a minute.

According to TxDOT documents, the project lowers the floodplain around it by roughly 350 acres and takes pressure off the Waller Creek tunnel. It's expected to finish around the end of the decade. One of the planned park decks over the highway lost a $105 million federal grant. The city put in $104 million for support structures, and the full set of decks would cost about $604 million. ## What this means for your home value in Austin

I'd be careful with anyone who says caves or the aquifer rules will raise or lower your price. Nobody has proven that. What I can say is that what's under the ground shapes what can be built next door, and a buyer's agent will look at the flood map for your address.

A few practical points for Austin sellers:

- **Flood maps.** In November 2025, FEMA released draft maps for Austin, Pflugerville, Round Rock, Manor and the rest of Travis County. As of November 2026, I haven't found a final version. Inside Austin, check your address on the city's map at ATXFloodPro.com. Elsewhere, search for FEMA's Flood Map Service Center.
- **Aquifer rules.** If you're in or near the Barton Springs Zone, check with the city before planning any addition, pool or driveway, and again before you tell a buyer what they can build.
- **Karst concerns.** If you've seen a sinkhole, a crack or a collapse on your lot, ask a qualified inspector or geotechnical engineer, and talk to an attorney about disclosure.
- **Competition.** In areas with few new homes, you're competing with your neighbors. In areas with lots of new construction, you're competing with builder inventory. My [how long it takes to sell a home in Austin](/blog/how-long-to-sell-a-home-in-austin/) post covers timing.

Most Austin homes sit on ordinary ground, and the pricing conversation is almost always about the street, the condition and the competition.
