Has the Austin housing market hit bottom? In the middle of the map, it might already have. Out in the suburbs, the data says not yet. On Zillow's home value index, the Austin area is still about 28% below its 2022 peak, but the city of Austin itself has stopped falling fast.

I'm an Austin real estate agent with eXp Realty, and I list homes across the metro. The question I hear most from sellers right now is whether to list or wait for the bottom. So I look at three things that tend to move before prices do: how many homes are for sale, how big the price drops are getting, and mortgage rates. Here's what they show as of November 2026.

If you'd rather skip to your own number, you can [get a free home evaluation](/home-value/) for your address.

## How far Austin fell, and where it slowed

Austin had one of the biggest drops of any big market in the country. In May 2026, ResiClub ranked the Austin metro first among the 15 large markets sitting at least 10% below their 2022 peaks. Its October analysis of Zillow's index put the Austin area at about 28% below the peak.

Now look at the city itself. Unlock MLS, the local MLS, reported that the median sale price in the city of Austin was up 4.2% in September from a year earlier. In October, it was flat at $550,000.

There's a second measure that surprised me. Redfin's home price index follows the same houses each time they sell again, so one big sale can't swing it. On that index, Austin was down just 0.2% in September from a year before. Houston was down 0.1%, Dallas 1.5% and San Antonio 2.1%. Austin fell harder than almost anywhere, and it's now among the flattest of the big Texas markets.

Here's the part I can say from my own work with resale homes in the city of Austin, without putting a number on it. Resale price per square foot fell sharply from spring 2022 into 2023. Since early 2024, it has moved in a narrow band. Over the summer it ran slightly above a year earlier for four straight months, June through September.

Think of a ball you drop on the floor. The first bounce is big, the next one is smaller, and after a few more it just rolls. The middle of Austin looks like it's starting to roll.

## Why the headline metro number jumps around

You might have seen a headline that Austin-area prices fell 6.9% in October. That's real. Unlock MLS put the metro median at $404,802, down 6.9% from October 2025. But the same report series had the metro median up 1.4% in September, at $421,000.

The metro median bounces with the mix of homes that happen to close in a month. It's the number everybody watches, and it's the last one to move. A number for the whole metro also can't tell you much about your street. If you want to see how your area compares, my [Austin seller page](/sell/austin/) explains how I price homes here.

## Signal 1: Homes for sale

Prices move last. Most sellers don't cut their price until they have to, so what changes first is how many homes are sitting on the market.

Think of it as a parking lot. New listings are cars pulling in, and sales are cars pulling out. When fewer cars pull in than pull out, the lot empties, and prices stop falling.

Here's October from Unlock MLS. Across the metro, new listings were down 14.3% from a year earlier. That's a lot fewer cars pulling in. But sales were down 11.4%, so the lot didn't empty. The metro had 6.2 months of supply, slightly more than a year ago.

In the city of Austin, new listings were down 19.4%. There were 7.9% fewer homes for sale than last October, and supply sat at 5.7 months, a little less than a year ago.

Out along SH 130, US 290 and I-35, the picture is different. In late November, Buda, Pflugerville, Hutto and Manor all had noticeably more homes for sale than a year earlier. In the middle of Austin, the lot is slowly emptying. Out in those suburbs, it's still filling up. If you own in one of them, my [Manor](/sell/manor/) and [Hutto](/sell/hutto/) seller pages cover what competing with that inventory looks like.

## Signal 2: The size of the drop

The second signal is how big each drop is compared with a year earlier. A single month can mislead, so I look at three months at a time.

Across the ten towns I track most closely, Austin included, the year-over-year gap in resale price per square foot narrowed through the first half of the year, getting close to flat in late spring and summer. Then it widened again in October. October closings are still posting, so that month can change.

The towns didn't move together. Pflugerville and Kyle improved from the spring into the fall. Manor and Buda got worse. Austin was slightly above last year in the spring and has been about flat since August.

The towns that are furthest from a floor have something in common. Kyle, Hutto, Manor and Buda are the four on my list where brand-new homes make up the biggest share of sales. Resale homes there compete directly with builders. If you're curious how that works in one of those towns, see the [Hutto new construction page](/new-construction/hutto/). I also covered the bigger picture in [why Austin suburb home prices keep dropping](/blog/why-austin-suburb-home-prices-are-dropping/).

## Signal 3: Mortgage rates

Rates aren't helping anybody right now. Freddie Mac's average 30-year rate was 7.21% in the release right before Thanksgiving. A year earlier, it was 6.23%, so rates are almost a full point higher than last fall.

The path this year matters. Rates dipped to 5.98% in late February, then climbed to a 2026 high of 7.40% in mid-October. The price gap was closest to flat a few months after that February low, and it widened again in October, the month rates peaked. I can't prove one caused the other, but I'm watching it.

Will rates fall next year? The big forecasters mostly say not much. Fannie Mae expects the 30-year rate to average 7.2% in 2027. The Mortgage Bankers Association has it between 7.1% and 7.3%. Goldman Sachs is more hopeful, at about 6.4% by the end of 2027.

Sellers don't often think about the other side of this. If rates fell fast, buyers would come back, and so would every neighbor who's been waiting to list. You'd be selling into a crowd.

There's also a slow change underneath. Realtor.com's analysis of FHFA data found that about half of all U.S. mortgages still carry a rate under 4%, but 22.5% are now at 6% or higher. That share could keep growing if rates stay above 6%. Owners with those loans give up less when they sell, so more of them can afford to move. That could bring both more buyers and more sellers.

## My read on the Austin home prices bottom

Put the three signals side by side. In the city of Austin, two of the three are leaning its way: fewer homes for sale than a year ago, and the drop has mostly stopped. In most suburbs, neither is there yet. Rates aren't helping anyone.

So here's my read, and it's a read, not a prediction. The middle of Austin looks close to its floor and may already be on it. For most suburbs, I think a 2027 story is more likely than a this-winter story.

The reason I say 2027 is builders. Over the past year, permits to build new housing in Austin fell 25.3%, the biggest annual drop among major markets according to Newsweek and CBS Austin. Matthews' Austin multifamily report puts the 2024 apartment completions peak at 32,074 units, and RealPage's schedule for 2026 shows about 10,300, roughly a third. A home that never gets started this year is one fewer competing with yours later.

The forecasters aren't calling a bottom either. Earlier this year, Zillow's forecasts had the Austin area falling another 4.6% to 6.1% over the following 12 months. And nobody can give you the month. A bottom only shows up when you look back at it, and anyone who hands you an exact date is guessing.

## What this means for your home value

It comes down to your timeline.

**If you have to sell in the next few months,** price for the market you're in, not the one you remember. Pricing is the biggest thing you control, and the first price is the one that matters most. My guide to [how long it takes to sell a home in Austin](/blog/how-long-to-sell-a-home-in-austin/) shows what overpricing does to your days on market.

**If you could wait a year,** waiting isn't free. Community Impact estimated that a $500,000 homestead in the city of Austin and Austin ISD pays about $8,215 a year in property taxes across the five taxing entities. Your bill will differ. The Texas Department of Insurance's preliminary figure for 2025 put the average homeowners insurance bill at $3,489. Together that's more than $11,000 a year before your mortgage. Waiting only pays if your home gains more than it costs to hold. My [Austin property tax guide](/blog/austin-property-tax-guide-2026/) walks through the tax side.

**If you've owned since 2020 or earlier,** you're most likely still well ahead. Crash is the wrong word for the middle of the city.

**If you bought in 2021 or 2022,** that's the harder spot. In many suburbs, the typical sale today is below what homes sold for at the peak. If you bought at the very top, you may have less equity than you expect, and the forecasts above don't point to a quick rebound.

Nobody rings a bell at the bottom. Your neighbors are reading the same headlines you are, and when the headlines say "recovery," a lot of owners who waited may list at the same time. That's not a reason to rush. It's a reason to know your number now so you can move when it makes sense for you.
